mIX

The mini system, running in this page

IX · The machine · What to try · Its speed · How it is in a page · t-IX, the tiny system

Each program of IX comes in two sizes. The mini ones, m-IX, are the faithful ones: Plan 9's programs written again in OCaml. Below, they run on a Raspberry Pi 1 that is itself one of them: mini-qemu, the emulator, compiled to JavaScript, boots mini-9pi, Plan 9's kernel in OCaml, from its SD card, with the shell, the utilities, the window system, the editors and the games on it. Everything on the board is IX's own code, compiled for ARM by its own compilers.

This is a first version, and it is slow: about a minute and a half to the shell's prompt, after 16 MB fetched. The tiny system's page, t-IX, starts at once.

What to try




The board has two ways in, as a real one has. Its serial
line is the text under the screen: just type, wherever the mouse
is, and the keys go there. Its screen has a USB keyboard and
a USB mouse: click the screen, and a blue frame says the keys and the
mouse are the board's (the browser keeps the pointer in it; Esc gives
it back, and a click outside the screen gives the keys back to the
serial line). The kernel prints its boot on both, then the shell's
prompt, %.



    

  • ls /progs, ls /progs/games — the programs of the card, by kind; ls /bin has them all.
    • 

  • ps, free, cat /dev/swap — the processes and the memory; ls /lib /src /usr/pad.
    • 

  • echo hello | tr a-z A-Z, seq 10 | sort -r, hoc, awk, grep, sed: the shell is mini-rc, Plan 9's rc.
    • 

  • rio, typed with the screen clicked — the window system. The right button gives its menu: New, then sweep a rectangle with the right button held, and a shell runs in the window.
    • 

  • tetris, emacs /src/hello.ml, office, page /lib/pdf/shapes.pdf, drscheme — in a window or on the bare screen. They take their time here.
    • 




Its speed




The line under the text says two numbers. The first is the ARM
instructions the emulator runs each second: a few millions in a
browser, where the same OCaml compiled for your machine runs twenty,
and a real Pi 1 some hundreds. The second is the board's clock
against yours: the board counts its time in instructions, so when it
is busy its second lasts longer than one of ours. What is written to
the card is kept in memory, and lost with the page.




Making it faster is what comes next: the emulator's loop first, then
perhaps the kernel itself compiled to JavaScript, with the browser
drawing its pixels.




How it is in a page



    

  • machine/ — the ARM processor: its instructions decoded and run, its memory, its MMU;
    • 

  • raspberry/ — the Pi's board around it: the interrupt controller, the timer, the serial lines, the SD card's controller, the framebuffer, the USB controller with a hub, a keyboard and a mouse;
    • 

  • MiniQemuWeb.ml — this page's part: the framebuffer on a canvas, the keys, the mouse, a slice of instructions at each frame. In a terminal the same board is mini-qemu, with QEMU's command line.
    • 




js_of_ocaml compiles

mini-9pi is
the kernel, and the card's programs are IX's

From a checkout, ./mini-pi -g mini-9pi runs the same

faster, and ./mini-pi -g -q mini-9pi under QEMU.
