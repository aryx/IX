IL, in a page

You typed "get il.txt". This text was not in the machine: it came over
its wire, from the other end, in two packets of data, because a
message of IL's is 1,024 bytes at most here and the text is longer.

What happened, a line a packet:

    > sync     "may I talk to your port 70?", and the number my
               messages will start from
    < sync     "yes", and the other end's number
    > ack      "heard"
    > data     the request, one line: il.txt
    < data     the text's first 1,024 bytes
    < data     the rest
    > ack      "I have both"
    < close    "I am done"
    > close    "so am I"
    < close    "heard"

Every packet of data has a number, one more than the one before, and
every packet says the last number its sender's program has read. So
each end knows what the other has, and who writes is never more than
20 messages ahead of who reads. A packet that comes twice is known by
its number and dropped; one that comes before another is kept until
the other arrives, and the program reads them in the order they were
written.

When the wire loses a packet, nothing is sent again blindly. The
sender, hearing nothing for a tenth of a second, asks: "I sent up to
7, where are you?" (query). The other end answers "I have up to 4"
(state). The sender then sends 5 again, and 6 after the next answer if
it is missing too. tiny-machine -net-lose 3 loses the third packet of
the other end, this text's second half: try it, the text is the same.

This is IL, the protocol Plan 9 had for its own traffic between its
machines (Presotto and Winterbottom). It is TCP thought again
for one use: messages, not a stream of bytes, so a read gives what a
write wrote and nothing has to be cut and put back together; a small
fixed window; no connection half closed. Its price: nothing on the
Internet speaks it, and Plan 9 itself went back to TCP.

In tiny-kernel it is TinyLibNetwork.ml, some 300 lines with IP's header
and the ping, and the other end of the wire runs the same file.
